Three-way matching, with a worked example and a live checker

Ordered 100. Received 96. Invoiced for 100. Three-way matching is the habit that catches that before the money leaves, and it needs no special software to start.

The short answer

Three-way matching means a supplier invoice is paid only after it agrees with two other documents: the purchase order (what you agreed to buy, and at what price) and the goods received note (what actually arrived). Two tests decide it:

Quantity billed is not more than quantity received. Price billed is not more than price ordered. Anything that fails is held, with a named person to chase it.

By Sayed Rashid, ex-auditor, 38 systems taken live. The method is the one I checked as an auditor and now set up in live systems. The two published figures on this page were checked on 11 November 2026 and are listed under Sources.

The worked example

Example figures
A packaging firm orders 100 rolls of film at S$12.00 a roll. The lorry brings 96. A week later the invoice arrives for 100 rolls, S$1,200.00 before GST.

Without the goods received note, the invoice looks perfect: right supplier, right price, right quantity against the order. The S$48.00 goes out and nobody ever knows. Now change one thing. Say the supplier billed the correct 96 rolls but at S$12.60. The quantity test passes and the price test fails: 96 x S$0.60 is S$57.60 over, and only the purchase order can show it. That is why it takes three documents and not two.

Try it with your own numbers

Pay S$1,152.00 of the S$1,200.00 billed. Hold or claim back S$48.00. Before GST.

The three documents, and who should own each

DocumentWho creates itWhat it provesUsual weak spot
Purchase order (PO)You, before the supplier actsWhat was agreed: item, quantity, price, termsOrders placed by phone or chat, with the PO written afterwards or never
Delivery order (DO)The supplier, travelling with the goodsWhat the supplier says they sentSigned at the door without counting
Goods received note (GRN)You, at the moment of receiptWhat your own person counted and acceptedRecorded days later from memory, or copied from the DO
Supplier invoiceThe supplierWhat they want to be paidEmailed straight to the person who placed the order

A delivery order and a goods received note are not the same thing. The delivery order is the supplier's claim. The goods received note is your count. In a small firm they can share one sheet of paper: the receiver writes the counted quantity on the supplier's delivery order, signs and dates it, and that marked-up copy becomes your receipt record. What matters is that the number was counted by your side.

Set it up in five steps

  1. No order number, no payment. Every purchase of goods gets a purchase order before the supplier acts, and suppliers are told to print that number on their invoices. A free purchase order generator is enough to begin.
  2. Count at the door. The receiver counts, writes the quantity on the delivery order, and records the receipt the same day. Not from memory on Friday.
  3. Match before you post. Accounts puts the three documents side by side, or lets the system do it, before the invoice is entered for payment.
  4. Write your tolerance down. Decide how small a price difference you will pay without asking, and who may approve anything above it. Start strict. You can loosen it once you see how often it trips.
  5. Give every mismatch an owner and a day. Held invoices go on one list that is cleared weekly. An invoice that sits unmatched for a month is a supplier relationship going sour.

Split the three jobs. The person who orders should not be the one who records the receipt, and neither should be the one who releases payment. In a team of three or four that is not always possible. The minimum that still works: whoever pays is never the person who ordered, and the owner looks at the held-invoice list once a week.

The reason auditors care about this control is simple. Each document is made by a different person at a different time, so a mistake or a made-up invoice has to get past all three to be paid.

Two-way, three-way or four-way?

MatchComparesUse it for
Two-wayInvoice against purchase orderServices, subscriptions and rent, where nothing is counted in at a door
Three-wayInvoice against purchase order and goods receivedStock, raw materials, packaging, spare parts, site materials
Four-wayAll of the above plus an inspection or acceptance recordGoods that can arrive in the right quantity and still be wrong: food, chemicals, made-to-drawing parts

Most small firms need three-way for anything that goes into stock or onto a job, and two-way for the rest. Do not put the office coffee through four-way matching.

What a wrong invoice costs

A study commissioned by IMDA estimated that errors affect 3% of paper invoices and can cost as much as S$72 each to put right, on top of about $8 of manual handling per invoice. Those are the study's figures, quoted in IMDA's InvoiceNow FAQ (updated 28 March 2023), not mine. The match is cheap by comparison: it is one look at three numbers.

It also gets easier as invoices start to arrive as data instead of PDFs, because a system can then do the comparing and show accounts only the ones that fail. How that delivery works is in How InvoiceNow works: one invoice, five stops.

Questions people ask

What is a three-way match, with an example?

It is a check that a supplier invoice agrees with the purchase order and the goods received note before it is paid. Example: 100 rolls ordered at S$12.00, 96 received, 100 invoiced. The invoice says S$1,200.00, the match says pay S$1,152.00 for the 96 that arrived and ask for a S$48.00 credit note.

What is the difference between two-way and three-way matching?

Two-way matching compares the invoice with the purchase order only, so it checks price and ordered quantity. Three-way matching adds the goods received note, so it also checks that what was billed actually arrived. Use two-way for services and three-way for physical goods.

Is a delivery order the same as a goods received note?

No. The delivery order is written by the supplier and says what they sent. The goods received note is your own record of what was counted and accepted. A small firm can use the supplier's delivery order as its receipt record, as long as its own receiver writes the counted quantity on it and signs.

What tolerance should I set?

There is no official figure. It is a business decision about how small a difference is not worth a phone call. Start at zero for price so you see every difference for a month, then set a small percentage or dollar limit, write down who can approve above it, and review it once a year.

What should I do when the invoice arrives before the goods?

Hold it unposted, or post it on hold, until the goods are counted in. Paying on the invoice alone is two-way matching by accident. If the supplier wants payment in advance, treat that as a deposit agreed on the purchase order, not as an early invoice.

Does a small company really need three-way matching?

For anything bought for stock or for a job, yes, because that is where short deliveries and price creep hide. It does not need software to begin: a numbered purchase order, a counted and signed delivery order, and a rule that accounts sees both before paying will do.
