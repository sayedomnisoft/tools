Most selections go wrong before the first demo

I have taken systems live and I have been called in to rescue ones that stalled. The stalled ones rarely chose the wrong software. They chose without a brief, compared quotes that were not quoting the same thing, and watched a demo of somebody else's business.

A system project is nine parts, not one: software, implementation, changes for your industry, data migration, links to other systems, training, your own people's time, support after go-live and a reserve. The parts most often missing from a proposal are the ones that stall projects later: data, links and your own people's time. (The nine parts)

So this kit works in the order that protects you: write the brief, read the quotes line by line, make the demo use your documents, then look at the whole cost.

Why this matters for a manufacturer or trader

Your business is the hard case for software: batches and recipes, multi-level bills of materials, landed cost, several stock locations, sometimes several companies. A proposal that reads well for a service firm can miss half of your day. (Data migration readiness test)

If a grant is part of the plan, the order of steps matters. The page on the EDGE grant explains the rule that catches people: apply first, then sign. (EDGE grant calculator and rules)

Ground rules for every promptUse a business account with data training switched off, or remove names first. Never paste NRIC numbers, salaries by name, bank account numbers or passwords. AI can miscount and can sound sure when it is wrong: check every figure against your own records before anyone acts on it. This is general guidance from experience, not legal, tax or financial advice.

Brief Team

Team 1 of 4 · 3 prompts

Goal: a brief any vendor can quote against, written as your real day, with every requirement sorted before anyone sees a demo.

Write our brief as one working day

You are helping a Singapore [manufacturer or trading company] write a brief for a new business system. Here is how one order moves through us today, from enquiry to cash, including the awkward cases: [describe one normal order and one difficult order, step by step, with who does what and in which file or system] 1. Rewrite this as "one working day": the 10 to 15 moments where information is typed, checked or passed on. 2. For each moment, write the requirement in one plain sentence that starts with "The system must". 3. Mark the 5 moments that cost us the most time or cause the most errors, and say why. 4. List what you still do not know and need me to answer. Do not add features we did not describe.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
the story of one real order, told the way you would tell a new hire. Roles, not names.
Good output
a two-page brief built from your day, not a list of 100 features.

Sort every requirement into 3 buckets

Here is our requirements list: [paste the "The system must" sentences] Sort each one into exactly one bucket and give a one-line reason: A. Standard: any mainstream business system does this by configuration. B. Work around: we could change our habit instead of the software. Say what the habit change is. C. Different: this is how our industry or our company really differs (for example batches, recipes, weighing, job costing, special approvals). Then list bucket C on its own, ranked by how much it matters to customers or to cost. If you are unsure, put it in a fourth list called "Ask the vendor" with the exact question to ask.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
the output of the first prompt.
Good output
a short bucket C. That short list is what you customise; everything else you configure or change.

Find the boring parts in our data

Act as a data lead before a system project. Our master data today: [number of products, customers, suppliers, bills of materials or recipes, price lists, and where each lives] Known problems: [duplicates, several names for one item, units, missing codes, old records] 1. List the data sets we must clean before any migration, in the order to do them. 2. For each, give a simple test of "clean" (for example: one record per customer, every product has a unit and a cost). 3. Say which of our people must own each data set, by role. 4. Estimate the effort as small, medium or large and say what would make it larger. Do not suggest software.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
counts and a frank description of the mess. No customer names needed.
Good output
an owner and a test for each data set. This is the part that stalls projects when nobody owns it.

Quote Team

Team 2 of 4 · 3 prompts

Goal: every proposal checked against the nine parts, and two or three quotes compared on the same lines. (source)

Check a proposal against the nine parts

Here is a proposal for a new business system: [paste the scope and price sections, with the vendor name removed] Check it against these nine parts of a system project: software; implementation; changes for how our industry works; data migration and cleaning; links to other systems; training; our own people's time; support after go-live; a reserve for the unexpected. For each part answer: is it in the proposal, is it priced, who does it, and what is assumed. Then list every assumption or exclusion in the proposal in plain words. End with the 5 questions I should send back before comparing it with anything else.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
the proposal text. Remove names if you prefer.
Good output
a nine-row table with the gaps obvious, and five questions to send back.

Compare 2 or 3 quotes line by line

I have [2 or 3] quotes for the same brief. Call them Vendor A, B and C. [paste each quote's line items, assumptions and exclusions] 1. Build one table with the nine parts as rows and the vendors as columns: what is included, the price if given, and the assumption behind it. 2. Mark every row where the vendors are not quoting the same thing, and explain the difference in one line. 3. Say which differences explain most of the gap in total price. 4. List what each vendor has left to us to do. Do not recommend a vendor. Do not estimate missing prices.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
the quotes with names replaced by A, B and C.
Good output
the reason one quote is double another, usually in the assumptions, not the software.

Questions for the vendor call

Write 12 questions for a first call with a system vendor, for a Singapore [manufacturer or trader] with [number] users. Cover: who will actually build and configure it (and may I meet them); one project of yours that stalled and what you changed after; how data migration is tested before go-live; how the cut-over weekend runs; what support looks like in month 2 and in year 2; how changes are priced after go-live; what you need from our people each week. For each question add one line: what a good answer sounds like, and what a warning sign sounds like.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
your size, industry and the two things that worry you most.
Good output
a one-page call sheet. The answer to "show me a project that stalled" tells you the most.

Demo Team

Team 3 of 4 · 2 prompts

Goal: a demo that runs your documents through the system, scored the same way for every vendor.

Turn our documents into a demo script

I will bring 3 real documents to every demo: [describe them, for example one sales order with a special price, one purchase with landed cost, one production or assembly job]. Write a demo script for the vendor to follow: 1. For each document, the exact steps we want to see on screen, from entry to accounts. 2. The awkward case to try in each (a part delivery, a return, a price change, a substitute item). 3. The 3 reports we want at the end, with the figure each should show. 4. What we will not accept as an answer ("that can be customised later" without a price). Keep it to 60 minutes.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
a short description of three real documents from last month.
Good output
a script you send a week before the demo. Vendors who prepare for it stand out at once.

Score the demo the same way each time

Build a demo scorecard for [number] people on our side (roles: [list]). Rows: each step in our demo script, plus data migration approach, reporting, ease for daily users, and how questions were handled. Columns: saw it working (yes or no), how many clicks or screens, who on our side would use it, score 1 to 5, notes. Add a rule: a step we did not see working scores 0, however good the explanation. End with a 5-line summary template each person fills in before talking to the others.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
your demo script and the roles attending.
Good output
one sheet per person, filled in before the group discusses. It stops the loudest voice deciding.

Money Team

Team 4 of 4 · 2 prompts

Goal: the whole cost over five years in one table, and the grant checked before anything is signed. (EDGE rules, with source and check date)

The whole cost over 5 years

Using only the figures I paste, build a 5-year cost table for each quote. [paste for each vendor: software or subscription per year, implementation, changes, data migration, links, training, support per year, and anything quoted as optional] Our own time: [hours per week from finance, sales and operations during the project, and a rough hourly cost] Rows: the nine parts. Columns: year 1 to year 5 and total. 1. Show one-off and yearly costs separately. 2. Add our own people's time as its own row. 3. Add a reserve row at the percentage I give you: [percentage]. 4. List every figure that is missing and who must supply it. Do not fill gaps with estimates.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
the numbers from the quotes. Leave blanks where a vendor gave none.
Good output
the line owners underestimate is rarely the licence. It is usually data, links and their own people's time.

Grant check before we sign

I am pasting the grant rules from an official page or from a page that quotes one, with its check date: [paste the rules from the EDGE grant page linked above, including what is not covered] Our plan: [what we are buying, from whom, when we intend to sign, other digital projects this year] Using only the pasted rules: 1. List each rule and whether our plan meets it, fails it or cannot be told yet. 2. Flag anything about the order of steps (applying, signing, paying). 3. List the documents we would need and who prepares each. 4. Write the 3 questions to confirm with the grant body or a pre-approved vendor. Do not state a grant amount unless the pasted rules and our figures give it exactly.
What to paste in, and what good looks like
Paste in
the rules as written on the page, and your plan.
Good output
a checklist that stops the common mistake: signing or paying before applying. The grant body decides, so confirm before you rely on it.

Before you sign anything

A kit can make a selection careful. It cannot tell you whether this is the right year to change system at all. Three signs it is not: nobody inside can give the project time each week, the master data has no owner, or the real problem is a habit that new software will not change.
