What a system project really consists of: the nine parts

For owners and finance heads holding a proposal, or about to ask for one. The nine parts every project has, whether or not they are written down, and a check that shows the gaps in yours.

The short answer

A system project is much more than software. It is nine parts: software, implementation, changes for your industry, data migration, links to other systems, training, your own people's time, support after go-live and a reserve for the unexpected.

The parts most often missing from a proposal are the ones that stall projects later: data, links and your own people's time.

By Sayed Rashid, ex-auditor, 38 systems taken live. Seven-plus years putting Singapore and regional firms onto one system, and rescuing projects that stalled.

The nine parts, and the question to ask about each

SoftwareLicences or subscriptions, and what changes when users or a second company are added.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
ImplementationWorkshops, setup, the project manager's time and testing support.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
Changes for how your industry worksBatches, recipes, weighing, job costing, approval rules the standard product does not have.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
Data migration and cleaningCleaning products, customers, suppliers and recipes; test loads; opening balances.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
Links to other systemsWeb store, marketplaces, payroll, bank files, in-house software, weighbridge or machines.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
TrainingKey users first, then everyone, in their own language where needed.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
Your own people's timeHours your finance, sales and operations people spend on workshops, cleaning and testing.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
Support after go-liveFixes, small changes and questions in the first months, when most come up.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?
A reserve for the unexpectedTime and budget held back for what nobody saw in the workshops.
  • Ask: is this in the proposal, and if not, who does it?

A real project, anonymised

A palm-oil plantation and mill group in Indonesia that outgrew off-the-shelf software. It got a custom mill module linked to the weighbridge and sensors, with staff trained in Bahasa.

The lesson: when the industry works differently, the system has to follow the industry, and the people using it have to be trained in their own language.

Five questions before you sign

  1. Which of the nine parts are in this proposal, and which are excluded in writing?
  2. Who cleans and loads our data, and how many test loads are included?
  3. Which links to our other systems are included, and who tests them?
  4. How much of our own people's time does your plan assume, and from whom?
  5. What does support after go-live cover, and for how long?

Questions

What does an ERP project consist of?

Nine parts: software, implementation, changes for how your industry works, data migration and cleaning, links to other systems, training, your own people's time, support after go-live and a reserve for the unexpected. Software is only one of them.

What is usually missing from an ERP proposal?

Data migration and cleaning, links to other systems, your own people's time, support after go-live and a reserve. These are the parts that stall projects later, so ask for each one to be included or excluded in writing, with who does it.

Is the software the biggest part of an ERP project?

Usually not in effort. Implementation, data work, links and your own people's time decide the outcome far more than the software choice does.

How much of our own staff time does an ERP project take?

More than most owners expect, and it is the part least often planned. Key users from finance, sales and operations spend time on workshops, cleaning data and testing for months; agree whose time, how many hours a week, and what they stop doing.
